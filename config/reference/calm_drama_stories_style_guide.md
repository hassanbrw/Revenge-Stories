Source: youtube.com/@CalmDramaStories (channel ID `UCJmBXfBrF6ybWLbpzN3JqXw`, 98.2K subs, 641 videos, joined 2025-11-14, 31.3M total views).

**This is the reference all future title, thumbnail, and story generation for this channel must strictly conform to.** Do NOT invent new antagonist types, settings, or themes for "variety" — stay inside this exact micro-niche. Every element below is drawn from real data: 60 real titles (top-viewed, sorted by popularity), 8 real downloaded thumbnail images (exact overlay text transcribed), and full-transcript analysis of the 10 highest-viewed videos (196K-300K views each).

## 1. Title Structure

**Formula:** `My [family member] [cruel/dismissive/humiliating act] — [em dash or "Until"/"So"/"Then"] — [time-skip reveal / consequence, left unresolved]`

Rules extracted from the corpus:
- Subject is always a first-person family relationship: **My Parents / My Family / My Dad / My Mom / My Sister / My Mother-in-Law / My Brother's Wife / My Stepmother**. Never a romantic partner as the primary betrayer (in-laws are the closest exception).
- Antagonist commits a **specific, concrete cruel or dismissive act** — not a vague "betrayed me." Real examples: ordered $4,000 of food and told her to pay; called her "the dumb one"/"uneducated and worthless"/"the maid"; sold her inheritance/grandma's house behind her back; excluded her from Christmas to sell a house; banned her from a wedding.
- A **specific number** appears in the vast majority of titles: a dollar figure ($4,000, $47M, $600,000, $120 steaks, $5 Million, $38K, $13M, $100M, $44M), a headcount, or a time span. Bare emotional claims without a number underperform in this corpus.
- **Time-skip twist** is extremely common: "9 Years Later," "5 Years Later," "7 Days Later," "2 Weeks Later," "15 Years Later." The gap itself is part of the hook.
- Title **never resolves** — it ends on a trailing clause ("...Now They're Calling 40 Times A Day," "...Then Told Me 'You're Paying,'" "...So I Grabbed The Mic") or a flat cut mid-action.
- The protagonist is consistently framed as secretly successful/wealthy/accomplished, unknown to the antagonist — this is baked into the title's implicit promise even when not stated outright.
- Common settings baked into titles: birthdays, weddings, funerals/wills, Christmas/Thanksgiving, graduations, baby showers, retirement parties, hospital/surgery moments, group chats.

## 2. Thumbnail Text — Real Measurements

Based on 8 actual downloaded thumbnail overlays (word-counted exactly), not assumption:

- **Average: ~55 words** (range 30-66 across the sample). This channel runs noticeably longer thumbnail text than a generic "40-50 words" guess — **update any prompt targeting 40-50 to 50-65, centered on ~55.**
- **Visual structure:** multi-color chunked/staccato lines — colors rotate per beat (white, yellow, green, magenta/pink, cyan), each color block is a short clause, NOT one continuous color. Final 1-2 lines get a highlight callout box (red or yellow background, bold) that is always the unresolved cliffhanger.
- **Opening line:** always a concrete scene-setter, never abstract — "AT MY 18TH BIRTHDAY PARTY, ...", "A YEAR AFTER THE DIVORCE, ...", "MY GRANDMA TURNED 79, BUT NO ONE CAME TO HER PARTY."
- **Middle:** escalating concrete details as short beats (numbers, dollar amounts, quoted cruel dialogue in "double quotes") — e.g. "16 PEOPLE. THEY'D ALREADY ORDERED. LOBSTER. WAGYU. A $400 BOTTLE OF WINE."
- **Dialogue quoting:** frequently includes 1-2 quoted lines — almost always the antagonist's cruel/dismissive words verbatim in quotes; sometimes also a short calm comeback from the protagonist.
- **Closing line:** always trails into an unresolved mid-action moment — "15 MINUTES LATER...", "THE NEXT DAY...", "5 MINUTES LATER, A MAN ENTERED THE ROOM... AND HER FACE LOST ALL COLOR." Never shows the actual outcome.
- **Phrase-lifting from story text:** the thumbnail text reads as if lifted near-verbatim from the video's own cold-open flash-forward beat (see Section 3 below) — same concrete numbers, same quoted villain line — reinforcing that thumbnail copy should be generated from the story's own opening hook, not written independently.

## 3. Story Structure, Tone, Pacing, Psychological Triggers, FOMO

*(Full analysis from a complete-transcript read of the 10 highest-viewed videos, 45-75 min each. This is the authoritative structural reference for `pipeline/story_generate.py`.)*

### Opening (universal 4-beat template)
1. **Identity stamp** — name, age, one compressed credential that pays off later ("My name is Diana. I'm 32 and I run operations for a restaurant group that seats 3,000 people a week...").
2. **Cold-open flash-forward** — jump straight to the climax's cruelest line, cut off before resolution (10-20 sec equivalent).
3. **Channel branding / CTA** — either a full tagline ("Welcome back to Calm Drama Stories, where ordinary people set quiet boundaries that change everything...") or a direct CTA ("Drop your city and local time below. I love reading those.").
4. **Transition phrase into backstory** — almost always "Now, let me take you back [N years] to..."

### Six-act structure (with runtime proportions)
| Act | Content | % runtime |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Cold Open | Hook + branding + CTA | 1-2% |
| 1. Origin/Setup | Childhood favoritism pattern, repeated small quantified cruelties, one secret ally (usually a grandparent), protagonist's rise begins | 20-25% |
| 2. Inciting Incident | Event that reopens contact/forces confrontation (dinner invite, retirement party, leaked group chat, ER intake) | 10-15% |
| 3. Investigation/Preparation | Protagonist quietly discovers the "receipts" (trust, deed, recording, folder, lawyer's letter) without tipping off the family | 15-20% |
| 4. Confrontation | Set-piece scene in front of witnesses; villain's signature cruel line repeated; reveal of document/truth; villain's public unraveling | 20-25% |
| 5. Fallout/Epilogue | Flat-toned consequence report; explicit one-line moral; fixed closing CTA | 15-20% |

**Mid-video engagement break** at ~40-55% mark: narration pauses, direct address to camera, word-prompt or multiple-choice tease, then explicit resume ("Now, back to the story."). Example prompts: "comment the word enough below," "type 'I see her' in the comments," "Option A... Option B... Option C. Drop your answer in the comments."

**Time-skip suspense devices**: explicit day/hour countdowns used as pacing markers ("Nine days. Day one of nine... Day four... Day five..."; "72 hours."; "23 days.").

### Psychological triggers (rotate through these, usually combining 3-4 per story)
1. Injustice/golden-child favoritism (explicit sibling-comparison with dollar figures attached)
2. Financial exploitation — protagonist cast as "family ATM"
3. Public humiliation (confrontations always happen in front of witnesses — 16-person dinner, 200-guest gala, courtroom, hospital hallway)
4. Being underestimated/intellectually dismissed ("the dumb one," "uneducated") — later revealed as the smartest/most successful
5. Erasure/disownment as identity theft (blocked numbers, cut from photos, "not one of us")
6. Vindication via institutional authority (a judge, bank, board, or hospital confirms the truth — not the protagonist yelling)
7. Poetic justice — antagonists undone by their own greed/scheme, never by direct protagonist revenge

**Leitmotif technique**: a cruel line said early by the villain gets thrown back at them verbatim at the climax. E.g. "You owe us that much." → "I don't owe you. I never did." Build this into every story's structure — plant a quotable villain line early, pay it off in the confrontation.

### Voice and tone
- "Calm" is tied explicitly to the protagonist's professional competence (surgeon, engineer, restaurant manager) — their career-trained composure is *why* they can stay calm under attack.
- Sentence rhythm: long expository/backstory sentences punctuated by extremely short (2-4 word) standalone emotional fragments on their own line: "Fine." / "Done." / "No." / "I don't owe you. I never did."
- Antagonist dialogue: short, declarative, entitled, delivered as if reasonable ("You're paying, right? You owe us that much.").
- Protagonist dialogue in confrontations is shorter than the antagonist's — power is shown through saying *less*, staying physically calm (sipping water while the villain shouts/cries), and letting documents/institutions do the talking.

### FOMO/retention mechanics
- Word-prompt engagement bait ("type the word boundaries below," "comment truth," "type karma")
- Location/time-stamp bait ("Drop your city and local time below.")
- Explicit "keep watching" cliffhanger lines before act breaks ("I just didn't know how right I would be.")
- Quantified suspense counters (days, hours, dollar amounts, call counts: "89 calls I let ring," "12 missed calls, eight text messages")
- False-choice/prediction device inviting viewers to guess before revealing

### Recurring plot elements
- Secretly successful/wealthy protagonist, hidden from family, revealed at climax
- Grandparent (usually grandmother) as secret architect/benefactor — sets up a trust/deed/will in advance, may be deceased, leaves a letter that surfaces at the right moment (present in 8/10 top videos)
- Legal-document "gotcha" — the reveal is always paperwork (trust, deed, quitclaim, power of attorney, board minutes, medical records), never just an argument
- Accidental family-group-chat leak revealing the plot against the protagonist
- A loyal outsider ally (waiter, CFO, spouse, lawyer) contrasted against the biological family
- Money offered is refused or given only with conditions — reinforces "this is a boundary, not revenge"
- Reconciliation (if any) only after success is undeniable, and remains partial/unresolved on the antagonist's side

### Ending structure (3-part epilogue, universal)
1. **Consequence report** — flat, documentary tone (job loss, foreclosure, divorce, reputation collapse), never gloating
2. **Explicit one-line moral/reflection** — e.g. "The truth doesn't shout and it doesn't chase. It just waits."
3. **Fixed closing CTA** — "That's my story. [numeric/symbolic recap tagline]. If this resonated, share it with someone who needs to hear it. Drop a comment, hit subscribe, and I'll see you in the next one." The recap tagline compresses the plot into a punchy list tied to a physical object introduced early (a glass of water, a manila folder, a brass key) that reappears in the final scene.

## 4. Protagonist Photo Style on Thumbnails

Checked directly against real downloaded thumbnails (hi-vis-vest dock supervisor, teal-scrubs ER doctor, white-coat physician, etc.):

- Chest-up photorealistic portrait, warm professional lighting, calm/closed-smile expression (matches the channel's "calm" branding — never exaggerated emotion).
- **Wardrobe and props are costumed to match the protagonist's profession in that specific story** — e.g. a hi-vis vest + "Supervisor" ID badge for a dock-worker story, teal scrubs + stethoscope for a doctor story. This is story-specific, not a generic headshot reused across videos.
- **The blurred background behind the sharp panel is a distinct contextual scene matching the story's setting** (blurred shipping containers/warehouse yard, blurred hospital corridor) — it is NOT the same portrait blown up and blurred, which is what our current pipeline does (`Thumbnail.tsx` reuses `photoSrc` for both the sharp panel and the full-bleed blurred background).
- **Gap vs. our pipeline:** we currently can't replicate the contextual-background technique without a generated (not user-supplied) photo, since the user's real protagonist photo has no matching profession-specific background to pull from. This is a candidate improvement for whenever the autonomous protagonist-photo-generation step (Google Flow / Nano Banana Pro) gets wired into `orchestrate.py` — at that point the photo prompt could explicitly request a contextual background matching the story's setting/profession, rather than a generic blurred studio backdrop.
