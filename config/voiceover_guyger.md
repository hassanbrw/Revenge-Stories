## [PART 0 — COLD OPEN]

She was a Dallas police officer. Sworn to protect. And on the night of September sixth, twenty-eighteen, she walked through a door that wasn't hers — and killed the man who lived behind it.

His name was Botham Jean. Twenty-six years old. Sitting in his own living room, eating a bowl of ice cream. He wasn't a suspect. He wasn't armed. He had done nothing wrong. He was simply home.

The officer said it was a mistake. That she thought his apartment was hers. That she thought he was the intruder. For thirteen months, that one sentence was her entire defense. But inside that courtroom, the mistake she kept explaining became the very thing that convicted her. This is how it happened.

## [PART 1 — WHO WAS BOTHAM JEAN]

Before this becomes a story about a police officer, it has to be a story about the man she killed — because the system almost let everyone forget him.

Botham Shem Jean grew up on the island of Saint Lucia. ⚠️(Confirm: Castries, Saint Lucia.) He came to the United States for college, graduated from Harding University, and built the kind of life most people only talk about. He worked as an accountant at one of the biggest firms in the world. He sang in his church. People who knew him describe the same thing again and again — a good man, a gentle man, deeply faithful.

By twenty-eighteen he was living in Dallas, Texas, on the fourth floor of an apartment complex called the South Side Flats. Apartment fourteen seventy-eight. A secure building. The kind of place you rent precisely so you can feel safe when you close your door at night.

On the evening of September sixth, there was nothing unusual. No crime. No emergency. Just a young man home alone, eating ice cream on his couch. Remember that image. Because everything that is about to happen happened to a man who had done absolutely nothing.

## [PART 2 — THE 911 CALL]

Amber Guyger had been a Dallas police officer for around four years. ⚠️(Confirm exact tenure.) That night she was coming off a long shift — still in her uniform, still armed. She drove into the same parking garage she always used. But she parked on the wrong floor. The fourth. One level above her own.

Every floor in that building mirrored the one beneath it. Same layout. Same doors. Same numbers, one digit apart. Her apartment was thirteen seventy-eight. His was fourteen seventy-eight. She walked to his door believing it was hers.

Minutes later, she called nine-one-one.

## [PART 5 — HER STATEMENTS AND THE EVIDENCE]

Her account was always the same. The door was ajar. The apartment was dark. She saw a figure, shouted commands, and when he moved, she fired twice. One shot missed. The other struck Botham Jean in the chest.

⚠️(Use trial testimony where she recounts this. If a released interrogation video exists, use it; otherwise use her sworn testimony.) But under questioning, the prosecutors did something simple and devastating. They took her own version — the one she had told over and over — and walked it backward, one step at a time. The training she had received on identifying a threat. The lit hallway. The red mat. The fact that her key never worked in that door, because it was never her door.

⚠️(Show the evidence exhibits entered at trial — her phone data and messages.) And there was more. Piece by piece, the accident began to look less like an accident and more like a series of choices.

## [PART 6 — THE TRIAL]

The defense had one strategy. Texas has what is called the Castle Doctrine — the right to defend your own home. They argued this was a tragedy, not a crime. That she genuinely believed she was defending her own apartment.

But there was one problem the law would not move around. It wasn't her home. You cannot claim the right to defend a home you entered by mistake. The very doctrine she was leaning on required the one thing she did not have — that the home be hers.

⚠️(Play the cross-examination. Let it run; cut in sparingly.) On the stand she cried. She apologized. She said she wished she were the one who had died. And the prosecution let her talk — because every time she explained the mistake, she reminded the jury of everything she ignored to make it.

## [PART 7 — THE VERDICT]

On October first, twenty-nineteen, the jury came back. Not manslaughter. Murder.

⚠️(Play the verdict clean. Do not talk over it.)

⚠️(Play the courtroom moment — Botham's brother, Brandt Jean. Let it run almost fully.) And then the courtroom did something no one was ready for. You can decide for yourself what justice looked like that day. Ten years for a life. A hug that split a nation in two. But one thing the law made permanent: a badge is not a key to any door you choose. And "I thought it was mine" is not a license to kill the man who was already home.
